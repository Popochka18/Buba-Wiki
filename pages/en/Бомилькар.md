## Personality

Bomilcar is described as a young man with eyes that "always sought the main chance" — an ambitious careerist who never misses an opportunity for his own advancement. Unlike the other officers — the loyal Mago, the calculating Hanno, the bewildered Adherbal and the inscrutable Himilco — Bomilcar is the only one who openly shows emotion: insolence at the council, then hysterical laughter, then naked hatred.

For all that, he is neither stupid nor cowardly — his accusations against Hannibal ("you cannot admit that you have lost") are essentially just, and Hannibal himself acknowledges it inwardly. Bomilcar is a product of the system he defends: a system in which strength and resolve matter more than principles, and a commander's hesitation is seen as a weakness to be removed.

## Role in the Council

When Hannibal's army, worn down by guerrilla war, dwindles from twenty to fifteen thousand men, it is Bomilcar who first voices what everyone is thinking — directly accusing the commander of being unwilling to admit defeat and insisting on an immediate retreat. He is not afraid to speak boldly even under threat from Mago ("Careful... you are speaking to..." — "I know who I am speaking to"), calling Hannibal "a legend that is killing us right now."

And when Hannibal, instead of retreating, proposes negotiating with Mateusz and granting Polonia its freedom, Bomilcar's reaction shifts from disbelief to open fury. He calls the proposal betrayal and treason, laughs hysterically at the thought that "the greatest commander of the empire wants to surrender to a bunch of peasants with pitchforks," and reaches for his sword — until Mago steps between them.

## Hannibal's Execution

Before dawn, Bomilcar returns to Hannibal's tent together with the four other officers — now with swords in hand. He formally declares an arrest "in the name of the empire and the regent Barkebal" for treason, though Hannibal himself understands that this is a lie: the messenger physically could not have reached Carthage and returned with an answer within a few hours. This is an execution, not an arrest.

Bomilcar strikes the first blow — the sword goes in beneath Hannibal's ribs. In that moment Hannibal sees on his young, handsome face not joy but fear — fear of what he has just done. Yet this does not stop Bomilcar: he wrenches the sword free and strikes again, and again, and again, until it is all over.

## After the Murder

That same day Bomilcar becomes commander of the army. He announces to the troops that Hannibal died heroically — by an enemy scout's arrow — and no one asks questions aloud (and those who do fall silent quickly). The new commander's very first order is a retreat south through the mountains — the very plan that Adherbal had originally proposed and that Hannibal had opposed. The text notes the irony: the plan for whose rejection Hannibal was effectively killed is in the end carried out by his murderer.

## Execution

After the destruction of the [[Хель Ливийская|Libyan Hel]], he was captured and publicly hanged in Krakow together with [[Ханно|Hanno]] and two other officers.
