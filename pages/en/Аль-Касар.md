**Al-Qasar** is the capital of Sardonia and the home city of Amir al-Faris. Once a formal ally of Carthage, the city — after a rebellion and a three-month siege under the command of Hannibal Barca — was taken by storm and sacked, turning from a living trade hub into "a city of which only ash and legends remained." At the close of the story, Amir returns there to rebuild it from the ruins.

## The City's Appearance (before the war)

In some ways Al-Qasar differed from the other cities Vladimir had seen along the way: instead of mud and squalor — paved streets with gutters for runoff, whitewashed façades, wooden shutters, lines of laundry strung between the rooftops. Above the western gate hung a crest — a falcon stooping on its prey, the symbol of Sardonia. At the gate stood Sardonian guards in green cloaks, not Carthaginian legionaries — an outward sign that Sardonia was considered an ally, not a colony. As Vladimir observes, "allies are allowed to think themselves free."

The closer to the centre, the richer the houses grew: white stone gave way to marble, the plain clothes of passers-by to silk and gold. The city had its caravanserais, markets and taverns; it was here, unloading goods at the port, that Vladimir and Urman found temporary work for a few weeks and gathered their strength for the journey onward into the mountains — to the house of al-Fahri.

## History

### The Rebellion of the "Children of the Falcon"

At some point the people of Al-Qasar rise up against the Carthaginian governor and wipe out the garrison entirely — "every last one, sparing not even the women and children," as the mercenary Arthur describes it. The rebels take for themselves the name "Children of the Falcon," after the city's crest; the marshal of the movement becomes Husid, Amir's childhood friend.

### Hannibal Barca's Siege

In response, Carthage sends an army led by Hannibal Barca — a commander who, by common account, "had never lost a single battle." The siege lasts three months. By the time of the final assault, mercenaries too are serving in the Carthaginian army — among them is Amir al-Faris, unaware (or not at first realizing) that he is storming the walls of his own home city. The mercenaries are promised double pay for the survivors and first claim to the spoils for those who first scale the walls — a wager on the riches of Al-Qasar, which even after three months of siege remained "a wealthy city."

The assault begins at dawn: drums, Hannibal's golden armour, walls glittering in the sun that "caught fire in the first rays of dawn, as if already wrapped in flame" — a detail foreshadowing what is to come. The defenders do not hold for long: by midday the walls had fallen, the gates flung open, and Al-Qasar — the city that for three months had withstood all the might of Carthage — was in the hands of the victors. In the final fight Amir kills Husid — without fully understanding whom he is killing, until it is too late.

### The Sack

After the city falls, the looting begins. Hannibal gives no order for violence — but neither does he forbid it, neither does he stop it, allowing the army "to do what armies do after a long siege": plunder, violence, death, slavery. The smoke over the city is visible for dozens of miles. Hasan later remembers that smoke as "black, thick, greasy — it rose to the sky as if the city were trying to smoke itself out of the memory of the gods." Hannibal himself afterward carries this destruction as one of the chief burdens of conscience that lead him to his final "choice."

### "Former Al-Qasar"

After the city's fall, Amir calls himself neither a prince nor a Pharisid, but simply "Amir al-Faris, of Al-Qasar. Former Al-Qasar" — a phrasing he repeats at every new meeting (with Janek, with Vladimir). That qualifier, "former," becomes part of his identity — a constant reminder that there is no longer a home to return to, only "ash and legends."

### Reconstruction

After the war's end — Hannibal's death, the fall of Carthage — Amir returns to Al-Qasar and begins to rebuild it, having renounced his titles ("just Amir, I am no lord"). At this point Farid comes to say farewell: he compares the rebuilding of his own people from the lands of Mur with the way Amir rebuilds Al-Qasar — "the way everyone rebuilds what was destroyed."

## Significance in the Narrative

Al-Qasar is, in essence, a personal version of the story's overarching theme: destruction that breeds new destruction (Amir, himself a victim of war, becomes its instrument against his own home), and the possibility of rebuilding once the cycle of violence is broken. The city travels a path from "an ally allowed to think itself free" — through the shattering of that illusion in the fire of the siege — to the final act of reconstruction, which for Amir becomes a form of atonement and the completion of his arc.
